Federer's forehand is built on an Eastern grip, one bevel flatter than the modern default. Where a Semi-Western or Western grip trades contact-point flexibility for topspin, Eastern keeps the racquet face nearly square through a wide range of arm positions — which is what lets this forehand be hit early, late, flat, or heavy without a grip change.

Preparation is compact and immediate: the unit turn starts the instant the ball leaves the opponent's strings, hands set high with only a short loop rather than a long wind-up. Because the grip does not need a deep drop to square the face, the swing can start small and still arrive on time — the single biggest reason this forehand can take the ball early.

Weight transfer is often linear rather than purely rotational. On a comfortable ball, the front foot steps in and the swing drives forward through contact, instead of relying on an open stance and pure hip rotation the way an extreme grip does. Power comes from stepping into the shot as much as from turning through it.

Contact happens earlier and lower than a Semi-Western forehand — around waist height, often before the ball has finished rising off the bounce. Taking the ball on the rise this way steals time from the opponent, but it only works because the flatter grip does not need extra height to get the face square.

The arm stays long through contact — a straight-arm lever rather than a bent, whippy one. A modern racquet-drop and forearm layback still happen in the transition from backswing to forward swing, adding racquet-head speed, but the extension through the ball is what keeps the shot flat and penetrating instead of steeply topspin-heavy.

The finish is versatile rather than fixed: a full, high finish over the opposite shoulder for a flat, driving ball down the line, or a shorter, wrapped finish when more topspin margin is needed crosscourt. An extreme grip locks the finish into one shape; Eastern lets the finish follow the shot.

Because Eastern sits close to Continental on the handle, the grip change to a serve, volley or slice is short — one bevel, not two or three. That is what makes this forehand the technical foundation for an aggressive, net-attacking, all-court game rather than a pure baseline one.

The trade-off is strike zone: Eastern gets uncomfortable on balls above the shoulder, so this style depends on taking the ball early and moving forward, rather than sitting back and letting heavy topspin balls rise into a high strike zone.
