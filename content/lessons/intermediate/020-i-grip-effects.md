A grip change is not a preference — it is a set of mechanical consequences. Rotate your hand one bevel and the racquet face angle at a given arm position changes, which forces the contact point, the swing path, the comfortable strike zone and the finish to move with it. Fighting those consequences is what makes a "grip change" feel impossible; accepting them makes it take a week.

Contact point. The further under the handle your hand goes, the further in front and higher contact has to be to arrive square. Continental contacts close to the body, Eastern beside the front hip, Semi-Western noticeably further forward, and Western further forward again. This is why a player who switches to Semi-Western without moving contact forward hits everything late and to the right (for a right-hander) — the mechanics are correct, the timing is not.

Swing path and spin. A closed-face grip needs a steeper low-to-high path to get the face square at contact, and that steeper path is exactly what produces spin. Eastern gives a shallow path and a flatter, more penetrating ball; Semi-Western a steeper path and heavy topspin with big net clearance; Western the steepest path, the heaviest spin and the highest bounce.

Strike zone. Each grip has a height at which it works easily. Continental and Eastern are comfortable from ankle to waist and get awkward above the shoulder. Semi-Western is comfortable from knee to shoulder. Western is comfortable from waist to above the shoulder and genuinely difficult on a low, skidding ball, which is why extreme-grip players slice low balls or take them early on the rise rather than trying to lift them.

Finish. Eastern finishes long and up over the opposite shoulder — the arm extends through the ball. Semi-Western commonly finishes across the body ("windshield wiper"), and Western wraps lower across the torso, because the forearm rotation that generates the spin also pulls the racquet around.

Grip-change speed. The cost of an extreme grip is transition time. Getting from Western to Continental for a volley is a long way around the handle, and there is no time to travel it during a fast net exchange. Doubles players and net-rushers often accept a flatter forehand to keep the grip change short; baseline grinders happily pay for spin with a slower change.

The practical takeaway: choose the grip that matches the strike zone you actually face. Play high-bouncing clay against loopy topspin? Semi-Western or Western pays. Play fast indoor courts against low slice, or serve and volley? Eastern earns its keep.
