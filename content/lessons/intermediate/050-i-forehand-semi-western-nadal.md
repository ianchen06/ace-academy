Rafael Nadal's forehand leans on the spin-generating end of Semi-Western, sometimes drifting toward Western on the most extreme topspin balls. The grip is what makes the rest of the technique possible: it rewards a steep, vertical swing path and a high strike zone, so every checkpoint below exists to generate and control spin rather than to flatten the ball out.

Preparation is a signature exaggerated loop: the racquet rises high, well above the shoulder, well before the forward swing starts, while the non-dominant arm stretches fully across the body for counterbalance and extra shoulder coil. This is a bigger, slower load than an Eastern forehand needs, because there is more rotational energy to store.

The stance is almost always open or semi-open, even on balls that could be taken neutrally. Nearly all the weight settles into the outside leg, and power comes vertically from leg drive and rotation rather than from stepping forward into the shot — the open stance is not a compromise here, it is the engine.

The swing path is steep and the forearm pronates hard through the brush, sweeping up and across the back of the ball. That combination is what produces extreme topspin: enormous net clearance on the way out, paired with a ball that drops sharply back down into the court instead of sailing long.

Contact is high and well out in front — often between waist and shoulder height, sometimes higher. This is a direct consequence of the grip: Semi-Western and Western both square the racquet face at a higher, more forward point than Eastern does, which is what lets this forehand attack balls that would crowd an Eastern-grip player.

The signature finish is the "buggy whip" or lasso: instead of wrapping across the body, the racquet whips up and over the same-side shoulder, especially on running or crosscourt balls. It is a variation, not the default — flatter, more aggressive down-the-line balls still finish with a more conventional windshield-wiper across the body.

The outside leg that loaded the shot also powers the recovery: the same explosive push that drives the swing snaps the player back toward the center, which is why this style is often paired with a visible hop or jump on the finish.

The cost of this grip is strike zone and transition speed: low, skidding balls and a fast grip change to Continental for net play are both harder from Semi-Western than from Eastern. This technique accepts that trade for topspin margin and a baseline game that dictates the point rather than moving forward to end it.
