The Semi-Western forehand (base knuckle on bevel 4) is the modern baseline default: heavy topspin, big margin over the net, and a comfortable strike zone up to shoulder height.
If you already have a clean Eastern forehand, this is a one-bevel move — but every checkpoint below shifts slightly with it, and skipping those shifts is what makes the change feel wrong.

Step 1 — Set the grip and check the face.
Base knuckle on bevel 4.
Hold the racquet out in front with a straight arm and look at the face: it should be angled slightly downward toward the ground.
That closed face is normal and is what the steeper swing path will open at contact.

Step 2 — Unit turn early, with a bigger coil.
Turn shoulders and hips together as before, but rotate a little further — the modern forehand stores more energy in the trunk.
Your non-dominant arm stretches across toward the sideline and acts as a counterbalance.

Step 3 — Choose the stance the ball dictates.
Neutral for a ball you step into, semi-open for a ball a step or two wide, open for a ball that pulls you off the court.
Semi-Western works from all three; the open stance is what lets you hit hard while moving sideways.

Step 4 — Load into the outside leg and keep the racquet head up.
As you set up, the hand is high and the racquet tip points up or back — a loop, not a straight take-back.
The loop lets gravity start the drop and keeps the arm relaxed.

Step 5 — Drop below the ball, deeper than for Eastern.
Let the racquet head fall well below the intended contact height — roughly two feet, more for heavy spin.
The bigger the drop, the steeper the upward path and the more spin you can generate.

Step 6 — Fire the kinetic chain and let the wrist lag.
Push off the outside leg, rotate hips then shoulders, and let the racquet trail behind your hand.
The lag is passive: you feel the racquet head being dragged, not pushed.

Step 7 — Brush up steeply through contact, well in front.
Contact is further forward and higher than Eastern — around waist to chest height, clearly ahead of your front hip.
The strings brush up the back of the ball rather than through it; think of the racquet travelling up the back of the ball toward the sky more than toward the target.

Step 8 — Windshield-wiper finish across the body.
Let the forearm rotate so the racquet sweeps up and across, finishing beside your opposite shoulder or lower across your torso.
Do not force it — the wiper is a consequence of the brush, not an extra action.

Step 9 — Recover with the outside leg.
The open stance leaves your weight loaded on the outside leg at the finish; push off it to recover.
Done well, the same leg that powered the shot starts the recovery, and you regain the middle a full step earlier.

Calibrating spin: aim for 5 to 8 feet of net clearance in rallies and a landing point 3 feet inside the baseline.
If the ball is clearing that high and landing short, you are brushing too much and driving too little — flatten the path slightly and extend more through the ball.
