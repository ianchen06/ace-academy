At advanced level, movement is rarely limited by top speed — the distances are too short for top speed to matter.
It is limited by three things: how quickly you start, how well you stop, and whether you recover to the correct place rather than simply to the middle.

The first step.
Split step timed to the opponent's contact, then a crossover step in the direction of the ball — not a shuffle, not a false step backward.
Elite movers appear to start before the ball is struck because they read the opponent's racquet face and shoulder line during the swing, so their split step lands with weight already leaning toward the likely direction.

Deceleration is a trained skill and it is where most injuries hide.
Arriving at a wide ball, the outside leg absorbs the load with a bent knee and the foot planted at an angle to the direction of travel.
Braking with a straight leg is both slower to reverse and the classic mechanism for knee and ankle injuries.

Sliding, on clay, is deceleration done better: you enter the slide with the outside foot, keep the trunk upright, hit during the slide, and finish with the outside leg loaded and ready to push back.
On hard courts, controlled sliding is possible but the surface grabs — treat it as an emergency skill, not a default.

Recovery is geometric, not habitual.
Recover to the bisector of the angles your opponent can hit from where they are standing — after a wide crosscourt ball, that point is not the centre mark but a step or two toward the side you hit to.
Recovering to the centre mark on every ball leaves you a step late on the most likely reply.

Depth of recovery matters as much as width.
If you hit a short defensive ball, recover further behind the baseline to buy time; if you hit a deep aggressive ball, recover on or inside the baseline so you are positioned to take the next one early.

Finally, movement quality collapses with fatigue long before speed does.
Conditioning for tennis means repeatable short efforts with short recoveries — sets of 6 to 10 second efforts with 20 to 30 seconds rest — because that is the actual work-to-rest structure of a point.
