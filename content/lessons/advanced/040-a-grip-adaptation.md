Every professional has a home forehand grip, and every professional leaves it several times a match.
The skill is not owning the most extreme grip you can control; it is recognising, early, when the ball in front of you calls for something else, and having the hand speed to get there.

Ball height decides most of it.
Above the shoulder, shifting toward Semi-Western or Western lets you attack rather than chip back.
Below the knee, shifting toward Eastern or Continental gets the face open enough to lift the ball, or to slice it deliberately.
A grip that never moves means one of those two balls is always a weakness.

Incoming spin matters as much as height.
Heavy topspin arrives high and kicking, which suits a more extreme grip.
Slice arrives low and skidding, which suits a flatter grip — this is precisely why slice remains effective against extreme-grip baseliners: it forces a grip change under time pressure, and most players do not make it.

Surface sets your baseline.
On clay, the ball sits up and rallies are long — extreme grips thrive.
On fast hard courts and indoor carpet, the ball stays low and time is short, so flatter grips and earlier contact points win.
Grass rewards Eastern and Continental strongly: low bounce, short points, and a lot of volleys.

Court position is the fourth input.
Deep behind the baseline, spin and margin are your friends.
Inside the baseline on a short ball, you want a flatter drive and a contact point you can step into, which usually means moving a bevel back.
Approaching the net, get to Continental during the approach shot, not after it.

Train grip changes as an explicit skill: the hand moves during the unit turn, using the non-dominant hand on the throat to hold the racquet while the dominant hand re-sets.
If you only ever change grips at walking pace during warm-up, you will not change them at all during a point.

One caution: adaptation is not indecision.
Your home grip should cover the large majority of rally balls, and the departures should be recognisable situations you have drilled — high ball, low ball, short ball, approach — rather than a continuous unconscious drift around the handle.
