The Western grip (base knuckle on bevel 5, palm nearly under the handle) produces the heaviest ball in tennis: extreme topspin, a bounce that jumps above shoulder height, and an angle that pulls opponents off the court.
It is a specialist tool.
On slow, high-bouncing courts against topspin it is a weapon; on fast low courts against slice it is a liability.
Learn it knowing both halves of that sentence.

Step 1 — Set the grip and accept the closed face.
Base knuckle on bevel 5.
Held out in front with a straight arm, the racquet face points noticeably at the ground.
Every other checkpoint below exists to open that face at exactly the right moment.

Step 2 — Turn early and coil hard.
The extreme grip requires a longer, steeper swing, which needs more time.
Start the unit turn on the opponent's contact, not on the bounce, and coil the trunk fully with the non-dominant arm stretched across.

Step 3 — Position deeper and take the ball at its peak.
Western grips are happiest contacting above waist and up to above shoulder height.
Position a step further back than a Semi-Western player would and let the ball come up into your strike zone rather than taking it early on the rise.

Step 4 — Load the outside leg and stay open.
Almost all Western forehands are hit from a semi-open or fully open stance; the grip does not lend itself to stepping across, and the rotation-plus-ground-force pattern is where the power comes from.

Step 5 — Take a compact high loop and drop deep.
Racquet head up in the loop, then a deep drop — further below contact than any other grip, because the path to the contact point is the steepest of all.

Step 6 — Fire hips and shoulders with a passive arm.
Legs drive, hips rotate, shoulders follow, and the arm is dragged through.
If you feel your arm working hard on a Western forehand, the chain has broken and you are about to hit the ball with your elbow rather than your body.

Step 7 — Contact high and far in front, with vertical racquet speed.
Contact happens well in front of the front hip and typically between chest and shoulder height.
The racquet is moving predominantly upward at contact — the ball is brushed, not driven, and the pace comes from spin plus rotation rather than from pushing through the ball.

Step 8 — Wrap the finish low across the body.
Forearm rotation carries the racquet up and then around, finishing across the torso or even near the opposite hip.
Do not manufacture the wrap; it is the tail end of the brush.

Step 9 — Recover immediately off the outside leg.
The extreme grip costs you time on the next ball, especially if it comes back low or short, so recovery has to be instant and you should already be planning to change grips.

Managing the trade-offs: on low balls, slice with a Continental instead of trying to lift them; on short balls, adjust one or two bevels back toward Semi-Western as you move forward; and never approach the net without changing grip on the way — arriving at the net still holding Western is how volleys get shanked.
